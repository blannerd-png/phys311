01 WHERE THE DECAYS START

Tracer concentration and the digital phantom

The body is built from 3D cylinders and spheres. Each region has a radioactive tracer concentration C, a material density, and an attenuation coefficient μ. The default phantom is a water cylinder with a spherical tumor whose tracer uptake is five times that of the surrounding body.

P(region i) ∝ Ci ViProbability of choosing an emitting region ∝ concentration × volume

Once a region is chosen, the code samples a uniformly distributed location within it, rejecting positions belonging to overlying regions. This produces more decays in a high-uptake tumor without manually placing extra photons there.

In the simulation: engine/phantom.js · sampleEmission()

02 RADIOACTIVITY

Poisson decay timing and the positron energy spectrum

Independent radioactive decays occur randomly in time. If the total activity is A decays per second, the time gap between successive decays is exponentially distributed.

A = λN,   λ = ln(2) / t½Δt = −ln(U) / AU is uniform on (0,1); in the code the simulated time is stored in nanoseconds

Each β⁺ decay emits a positron with a range of possible kinetic energies rather than a single energy. The simulation uses a simplified allowed β-spectrum:

f(T) ∝ p(T) [T + mec²] (Tmax − T)²pc = √[T(T + 2mec²)]T: positron kinetic energy; mec² = 0.511 MeV; endpoint Tmax depends on the isotope

The Fermi function is omitted, and the activity stays fixed during a short acquisition. The isotope selection controls the endpoint and the positron-range calibration.

In the simulation: engine/scanner.js · acquire(); engine/positron.js · makeBetaSampler()

03 CHARGED-PARTICLE TRANSPORT

Positron slowing, scattering, and range

Before annihilation, the emitted positron travels through matter, loses energy, and changes direction due to many Coulomb interactions. The code uses the Lab 9 Body → World → EulerCromer engine, with a simplified Bethe-like slowing force and random angular kicks.

Fstop = − b m v / |v|³vn+1 = vn + (Fn/m) Δtrn+1 = rn + vn+1 ΔtThe force magnitude scales as 1/v²; random direction changes use θ ∼ Normal(0, 0.35²) per integration step

This is a calibrated transport approximation, not a full relativistic electron transport code. Its displacement distribution is scaled to match mean positron ranges in water. The actual track is computed in a plane and randomly oriented in 3D.

IsotopeEndpoint TmaxMean range in water
F-180.634 MeV0.57 mm
C-110.960 MeV1.02 mm
Ga-681.899 MeV2.69 mm
Rb-823.378 MeV5.33 mm

Why it matters: the positron usually annihilates away from its original decay point. Longer positron range blurs small structures in the reconstructed PET image.

In the simulation: engine/positron.js · StoppingForce, ScatteringForce, run3D()

04 RELATIVISTIC KINEMATICS

Electron–positron annihilation and conservation of four-momentum

Once slowed, the positron annihilates with an electron. If their total momentum is zero, two gamma photons emerge in exactly opposite directions with energy 511 keV each.

e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γEγ1 = Eγ2 = mec² = 511 keVEγ1 + Eγ2 = Epairpγ1 + pγ2 = ppair

Real pairs can have a small residual momentum. The model samples each component of the pair momentum from a Gaussian distribution with standard deviation 1.9 keV/c, then applies a Lorentz boost from the pair rest frame:

Epair = √[(1022 keV)² + (pc)²]β = pc / Epair,   γ = Epair / 1022 keVE′γ = γ(Eγ + βcp∥)The result is ~0.5° FWHM non-collinearity in one projected direction

The exact boosted photons still conserve total energy and momentum. Their tiny deviation from 180° adds another source of PET spatial blur.

In the simulation: engine/annihilation.js · annihilate(); conservation checked in tests.mjs

05 GAMMA-RAY TRANSPORT

Straight-line propagation and Beer–Lambert attenuation

Gamma photons move at the speed of light along rays. With Compton scatter disabled (the baseline MUST model), a photon either survives along its original path or is removed by an interaction in the body.

r(s) = r0 + s d̂tflight = L / cPsurvive = exp[−∫ray μ(r) ds]c = 29.9792458 cm/ns; μ is the material-dependent linear attenuation coefficient

Attenuation coefficients used for the 511 keV phantom:

Materialμ (cm⁻¹)Meaning

Bone0.172Stronger attenuation

A photon must also intersect the finite detector cylinder; rays escaping past the axial ring stack are not recorded.

06 OPTIONAL EXTENSION

Compton scattering and photon energy loss

When you enable Compton scatter, a photon can change direction and lose energy before reaching a detector. The scattered photon obeys the Compton energy-angle relationship:

E′ = E / [1 + (E / mec²)(1 − cos θ)]For E = 511 keV and θ = 90°, E′ = 255.5 keV

Scattering angles follow the Klein–Nishina distribution rather than being uniformly random:

dσ/dΩ ∝ (E′/E)² [E′/E + E/E′ − sin²θ]

The simulation uses Woodcock (delta) tracking to sample interactions in regions with different attenuation coefficients, then applies the Compton formula at accepted interactions. Scattered photons may be rejected by the energy window. Photons that fall below 20 keV are treated as absorbed.

In the simulation: engine/photon.js · sampleKleinNishina(), comptonEnergy(), transportPhoton()

07 DETECTOR RESPONSE

Crystal efficiency, energy resolution, and the energy window

A photon that reaches a detector crystal is independently accepted with probability ε. If both photons reach the crystals, and no other losses apply, the probability of recording both is ε².

Ptwo detections = ε²Emeasured = Etrue + σEZσE = [r / 2.355] √[(511 keV) Etrue]Z ∼ Normal(0,1), r is the fractional energy FWHM at 511 keV

With default settings, ε = 0.80, so ideal two-photon detection is 0.80² = 0.64. The energy resolution is 12% FWHM at 511 keV; only energies in the 425–650 keV acceptance window are eligible for a coincidence.

Important distinction: 64% does not mean 64% of all decays become image events. Photons must also escape the body, reach the finite ring stack, pass the energy window, and survive coincidence sorting.

In the simulation: engine/scanner.js · decay()

08 EVENT SORTING

Coincidence timing, true pairs, scatter, and randoms

Every recorded photon receives a timestamp composed of its decay time, travel time, and Gaussian detector jitter. The two detector timestamps are compared within a coincidence window.

tmeasured = tdecay + L/c + δtσsingle = CTR / (2.355√2)τwindow = 2RFOV/c + 2·CTRCTR is the FWHM of the difference between two photon timestamps; use consistent ns units for τ

At the default CTR of 400 ps and field-of-view radius 14 cm, the automatic coincidence window is about 1.73 ns. A window with exactly two singles becomes a prompt; more than two singles are rejected as a multiple.

  • True: both singles came from one annihilation and neither photon scattered.
  • Scatter: one annihilation, but at least one photon scattered.
  • Random: singles from different decays were accidentally paired.
NECRmodel = T² / [(T + S + R)·t]T, S, R are recorded true/scattered/random counts in acquisition time t; this is the model's simplified NECR

In the simulation: engine/scanner.js · sortCoincidences(), makeEvent(), stats()

09 3D COVERAGE

Detector rings, bed stepping, and lines of response

The scanner is a stack of circular detector rings. A valid coincidence defines a line of response (LOR) connecting two crystal centers. Its location is initially ambiguous along that line.

Laxial = Nrings × pitchzSSRB = (z1 + z2)/2s = x cosφ + y sinφSSRB is single-slice rebinning: an oblique 3D LOR is assigned to an axial slice

Default hardware: 24 rings, 0.5 cm per ring (12 cm axial field of view), 512 crystals per ring, and a 40 cm detector radius. The accepted ring difference controls the difference between 2D and fully 3D detection. Moving the bed changes the region of the patient inside the detector field of view.

Why it matters: a lesion can be completely missed if it is outside the axial field of view, even when the detectors have excellent time resolution and efficiency.

In the simulation: engine/scanner.js · crystalCenter(), bedOffsets; recon/reconstruction.js · add()

10 INVERSE PROBLEM

Sinograms, backprojection, filtered backprojection, and MLEM

The scanner measures lines rather than a picture. Each LOR contributes to a sinogram indexed by angle φ and signed distance s. Reconstruction estimates the original tracer distribution from these many projections.

p(φ,s) = ∬ f(x,y) δ(s − x cosφ − y sinφ) dx dyThe Radon-transform relationship between a true activity image and ideal line-integral measurements

Backprojection

Each measured line is spread back across image pixels; without filtering, the result is blurry.

fBP(x,y) = ∫0π p(φ, x cosφ + y sinφ) dφ

Filtered backprojection (FBP)

Before backprojecting, the sinogram is convolved with a Shepp–Logan-filtered ramp to reduce the blur inherent in simple backprojection. The final image also receives a 0.6 cm FWHM Gaussian smoothing operation.

fFBP = BP(h * p)h: reconstruction filter kernel; *: convolution along detector coordinate s

Maximum-likelihood expectation maximization (MLEM)

MLEM starts with an estimate of the image, predicts the measured sinogram, and iteratively adjusts pixel intensities so the prediction better matches the observations.

xj(k+1) = [xj(k) / ∑iaij] · ∑i[aijyi / (Ax(k))i]y: measured counts; A: projection operator; x: reconstructed pixel intensities; k: iteration

The attenuation-correction option uses the known phantom μ-map and factors derived from exp(+∫μ ds). This is a simplified PET/CT-style correction, not a full clinical correction pipeline.

In the simulation: recon/reconstruction.js · fbpFromSino(), mlemSlab(), listModeMLEM()

11 TIME-OF-FLIGHT PET

Using a timing difference to locate the annihilation

A normal coincidence indicates which line contained the annihilation. Time-of-flight (TOF) PET uses the difference in the two photon arrival times to estimate where along that line the event originated.

Δx = c Δt / 2FWHMposition = c·CTR / 2σposition = c·CTR / (2 × 2.355)For CTR = 400 ps: spatial FWHM ≈ 6.0 cm and σ ≈ 2.55 cm along the LOR

TOF backprojection distributes each event along its LOR using a Gaussian centered at the estimated position. TOF list-mode MLEM uses the same Gaussian inside an iterative reconstruction. Better CTR narrows the spatial weighting and can reduce background noise.

Subtle point: timing resolution does not by itself make the crystals smaller or change the non-TOF system's intrinsic geometric blur. Its additional spatial information comes from using Δt in TOF reconstruction.

In the simulation: recon/reconstruction.js · tofSigma(), tofBackprojection(), listModeMLEM()

12 THE PROJECT'S MAIN QUESTION

Can the scanner actually detect the tumor?

Producing a picture is not enough: the simulation tests whether a hot tumor stands out statistically above background noise. It measures the average reconstructed tumor intensity and compares it to several equal-size background regions of interest (ROIs).

CNR = (μtumor ROI − μbackground ROIs) / σbackground ROI meansHere μ denotes a mean pixel intensity, not the attenuation coefficient used earlier
Rose criterionCNR ≥ 5 → detectable in this simulation

The live readout reconstructs a 1 cm slab through the tumor center with 6 iterations of MLEM, then evaluates the ROIs. Change efficiency, CTR, scatter, lesion uptake, or axial coverage to see whether detectability improves or worsens. Results have Monte Carlo uncertainty and should not be treated as clinical thresholds.

In the simulation: recon/detectability.js · lesionCNR(); main.js · measureCnr()

13 HOW TO INTERPRET THIS MODEL

Numerical methods, assumptions, and source files

Simulation numerics

Radioactive events are sampled with a seeded pseudo-random number generator. The browser uses a fixed-step accumulator (1/60 second of simulation-clock update per tick) so acquisition steps do not depend on monitor refresh rate. Positron trajectories use Euler–Cromer numerical integration; photons are ray-traced analytically until an interaction or the detector boundary. Reconstruction operates on a pixel grid and discrete line bins.

Important approximations

  • Activity is treated as constant over the simulated acquisition; nuclear daughters and biological uptake kinetics are not modeled.
  • Positron stopping and scattering are tuned simplified forces, not Geant4-level particle transport.
  • The phantom is idealized geometry rather than a patient CT scan; detector crystals are simplified acceptance elements.
  • No detector dead time, crystal-depth interactions, normalization, or clinical scatter/random correction.
  • Single-slice rebinning approximates oblique 3D lines; the live CNR is a simulation-specific decision metric.
