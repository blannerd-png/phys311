# PHYS 311 — Final reflection (draft for personal review)

November 26, 2026

I built an interactive simulation of a positron emission tomography (PET) scanner to explore the relationship between the physical behavior of a radioactive tracer, detector performance, and the quality of a reconstructed medical image. I wanted to create something that would help someone learning medical physics visualize a process that is difficult to understand solely from equations. The user can change the size and uptake of a simulated tumor, vary detector efficiency and coincidence timing resolution, and observe whether the tumor becomes distinguishable from background activity. The simulator includes three-dimensional photon transport, positron range, relativistic annihilation, attenuation, coincidence sorting, and several reconstruction methods. The project is an educational model, not a clinical scanner.

The final version differs from the Week 11 proposal in both its scientific focus and presentation. The original proposal emphasized the physical chain from positron emission to image formation, along with a half-life timer, isotope branching fractions, and validation. The final version emphasizes the detector-performance question: what efficiency and timing resolution are needed to distinguish a 3D tumor under specified acquisition conditions? It also includes time-of-flight reconstruction, variable axial coverage, bed stepping and optional Compton scattering. As documented in DECISIONS.md, dynamic half-life, branching fractions, and an additional Cu-64 preset were deferred rather than added at the expense of testing. The new interface places the primary experiment first, and the advanced features are still present but are revealed only when needed. I learned that adding physics features and explaining physics are not necessarily the same thing.

One difficult problem during the semester was getting the simulation to initialize reliably after adding the separate physics explanation view. A blank scanner could appear even though the HTML page loaded. The earlier project troubleshooting identified a risk with mixing files from different versions: the main JavaScript file depends on separate engine, rendering, reconstruction and runtime modules, and the page also needs to be served over HTTP rather than opened directly as a file. I added clearer startup diagnostics and kept the two views in one page so the scanner state could be preserved when switching tabs. The Week 13 archive retains the complete dependency structure. This experience reinforced the importance of checking the browser console and following the module dependency chain instead of assuming a rendering problem means there is an error in the physics. Another important finding came from the numerical convergence study. In the deterministic stopping-force reduction, Euler-Cromer gave approximately first-order convergence. At the finest tested timestep, the numerical displacement differed from the analytic displacement by about 0.014%. The study also revealed a limitation in the combined scattering and stopping approximation. Under deliberately extreme scattering angles, simultaneous explicit velocity updates can produce a small artificial energy increase. I documented this result instead of claiming that the method always behaves correctly. In contrast, the exact relativistic annihilation test achieved energy closure near floating-point precision. These two results illustrate why it matters to test each subsystem using the appropriate physical observable instead of applying one general conservation claim to the entire simulator.

If I had another month, I would start by separating the stopping and scattering updates so that changes in direction cannot artificially add kinetic energy during dissipation. I would recalibrate and validate the positron range distribution after making that change, rather than assuming previous range results still apply. I would also implement isotope-specific half-life and branching fractions, repeat the Rb-82 half-life validation, and add detector dead time. On the imaging side, I would study how reconstruction and random-coincidence corrections influence the CNR thresholds using longer acquisitions and more independent seeds. Finally, I would benchmark the same configurations on different devices to identify a practical performance limit rather than relying on how the application feels on one computer.

At the beginning of the semester, checking a falling ball against y = ½gt² seemed like a straightforward exercise. I now understand that the same principle applies to a much larger numerical model. A convincing simulation needs more than an attractive image or a physically plausible animation. It requires a clearly defined model, units, an appropriate numerical method, controlled initial conditions, independent analytic comparisons, convergence studies, reproducible numerical results, and an honest description of its limitations. I also understand the difference between discretization error and Monte Carlo uncertainty: decreasing a timestep improves a deterministic integration error, whereas increasing the number of sampled events reduces statistical noise. The PET project helped me connect computational physics with my interest in medical physics and understand how the physical limitations of a detector influence an image. The most important thing I can communicate is not that the model is perfect, but what it can predict, how I tested it, and where those predictions stop being reliable.
