What surprised me most about designing for a user rather than myself was how much I had to consider beyond whether the code actually worked. I had to think about how someone unfamiliar with my simulation would understand the controls, what features would be useful, and how to prevent users from accidentally causing problems. For example, I realized that allowing users to change certain parameters in every scene could cause numerical instability or make the simulation behave in ways that weren't helpful. I ended up disabling some sliders depending on the scene to make the interface more intuitive. I also hadn't considered how important accessibility, keyboard controls, and responsive resizing were until this lab. For my final project, I want to create a particle simulation. Since I'm studying nuclear engineering and physics, I think it would be interesting to simulate how particles move and interact with different materials. I could potentially incorporate particle collisions, scattering, and energy loss to demonstrate some of the physics involved in radiation transport. However, I realize that accurately simulating particle interactions can become very complicated, so I would probably have to make quite a few assumptions to simplify the physics and keep the code manageable. For example, I might have to limit the types of particles, interactions, or materials being modeled. I haven't decided exactly how detailed I want to make it yet, but I think it would be an interesting way to connect computational physics to my interests in nuclear engineering.
